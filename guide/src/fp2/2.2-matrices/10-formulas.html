Mx=b
3 equations = 1 matrix equation
$$\begin{aligned}a_1x+b_1y+c_1z&=d_1\\a_2x+b_2y+c_2z&=d_2\\a_3x+b_3y+c_3z&=d_3\end{aligned}\iff\begin{pmatrix}a_1&b_1&c_1\\a_2&b_2&c_2\\a_3&b_3&c_3\end{pmatrix}\cvec{x}{y}{z}=\cvec{d_1}{d_2}{d_3}$$
$$\det M\ne0\iff\text{unique solution}\iff\text{3 planes meet at 1 point}$$

Left-side numbers, row by row, make the matrix. Right sides make the column.

Determinant not 0 ⇔ exactly one solution ⇔ three planes cross at one point.

Determinant 0 ⇒ no solutions or infinitely many: never exactly one.

  1. Determinant not 0 ⇒ inverse exists ⇒ multiply both sides by it on the left ⇒ one answer, $\cvec xyz=M^{-1}\cvec{d_1}{d_2}{d_3}$.
  2. Each equation is a plane (row of left-side numbers = plane normal). One answer = one point on all three.
planes
det = 0: which picture
$$\begin{array}{l|l|l}\det M=0 & \text{no 2 planes parallel} & \text{2 planes parallel}\\\hline\text{consistent} & \text{sheaf: 3 planes share 1 line} & \text{2 planes identical, 3rd cuts them in a line}\\\text{inconsistent} & \text{triangular prism} & \text{2 parallel planes (not identical), 3rd cuts both}\end{array}$$

Parallel ⇔ rows of left-side numbers are multiples. Identical ⇔ whole equations (right side too) are multiples.

Consistent = at least one solution. Inconsistent = none.

Consistent, no parallel pair ⇒ all three planes hinge on one line, like pages of a book (sheaf).

Inconsistent, no parallel pair ⇒ each pair meets in a line, the three lines are parallel, no common point (prism).

Learn it: name the picture from the table.

Ae=λe
Eigenvalue, eigenvector
$$A\V e=\lambda\V e,\qquad \V e\ne\V 0$$

Any nonzero multiple of $\V e$ is also an eigenvector, same $\lambda$.

Matrix times eigenvector = number times same eigenvector. Number = eigenvalue.

Picture: matrix only stretches the eigenvector (or flips it), never turns it off its line.

det(A−λI)
Characteristic equation
$$\det(A-\lambda I)=0$$

Triangular (all zeros below diagonal, or all above) ⇒ eigenvalues = diagonal entries.

Take $\lambda$ off every diagonal entry. Determinant of that = 0. Roots = eigenvalues.

  1. Eigen equation $A\V e=\lambda\V e$ ⇒ $(A-\lambda I)\V e=\V 0$.
  2. Nonzero eigenvector solves it ⇒ not the unique solution $\V 0$ ⇒ determinant of $A-\lambda I$ = 0.
row×row
Eigenvector from rows
$$\V e=(\text{row } i\text{ of }A-\lambda I)\times(\text{row } j\text{ of }A-\lambda I)\qquad 2\times2:\ \text{row }(p\ \ q)\Rightarrow\V e=\begin{pmatrix}q\\-p\end{pmatrix}$$

Pick 2 rows that are not multiples (and not zero). Answer $\V 0$ ⇒ picked a bad pair, try another.

Eigenvector is ⊥ every row of $A-\lambda I$. Cross product of two rows is ⊥ both ⇒ eigenvector.

Picture: rows are two arrows; eigenvector sticks straight out of the flat they span.

  1. $(A-\lambda I)\V e=\V 0$ row by row: each row · eigenvector = 0 ⇒ eigenvector ⊥ each row.
  2. Vector ⊥ two given arrows = their cross product (or a multiple).
PDP⁻¹
Diagonal form
$$A=PDP^{-1},\qquad A^n=PD^nP^{-1},\qquad f(A)=P\,f(D)\,P^{-1}$$
$$P=\big(\V e_1\ \ \V e_2\ \ \V e_3\big),\qquad D=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&0&0\\0&\lambda_2&0\\0&0&\lambda_3\end{pmatrix}$$

Same order in $P$ and $D$. $f(D)$: apply $f$ to each diagonal entry, e.g. $A^{-1}\to\frac1\lambda$, $14A+24I\to14\lambda+24$.

Eigenvectors side by side = $P$. Eigenvalues down the diagonal, same order = $D$.

Power (or inverse, or any polynomial) of $A$ ⇒ do it to the diagonal numbers only; $P$ stays.

  1. $A$ times each eigenvector column = eigenvalue × that column ⇒ $AP=PD$ ⇒ $A=PDP^{-1}$.
  2. $A^2=PDP^{-1}PDP^{-1}=PD^2P^{-1}$: middle $P^{-1}P$ cancels. Same for any power.
C–H
Cayley–Hamilton
$$\lambda^3+a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0\;\Rightarrow\;A^3+aA^2+bA+cI=O$$

Matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. Swap $\lambda\to A$; plain number $c\to cI$; right side 0 → zero matrix.

Learn it: proof not needed.

f(λ)
Eigenvalues of related matrices
$$A\V e=\lambda\V e,\ B\V e=\mu\V e\;\Rightarrow\;\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}A^n & A^{-1} & A+kI & AB & A+B\\\hline\lambda^n & \frac1\lambda & \lambda+k & \lambda\mu & \lambda+\mu\end{array}$$

Same eigenvector $\V e$ every time.

Whatever you do to $A$, do to $\lambda$. Eigenvector stays.

  1. Start $A\V e=\lambda\V e$. Multiply on left by $A$ (or $A^{-1}$, $B$).
  2. Swap each $A\V e$ for $\lambda\V e$; number $\lambda$ slides to the front.
  3. End: (new matrix)$\,\V e$ = (number)$\,\V e$.
